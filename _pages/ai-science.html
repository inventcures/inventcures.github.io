A living reading list / Ashish Makani

AI × Science.

Better questions. Reliable discoveries.
Human agency at the center.

The future I want
to help build.

I want a future in which AI agents help people ask better questions, explore more possibilities and turn reliable discoveries into benefits that reach more people. Science and mathematics offer extraordinary opportunities for this kind of collaboration, especially in medicine and biology, where better understanding can ultimately mean less suffering and more healthy life. I am interested in human–AI teams and collaborations across laboratories around the world, with each contributing strengths the others lack.

Progress should deepen human agency and meaning: the freedom to choose what matters, understand the work, challenge an answer and share in discovery. That means evaluating evidence rather than just fluent output, preserving uncertainty and giving people real authority over consequential decisions. It also means designing against accidental harm and deliberate misuse, taking dual-use risks seriously, and making the benefits of scientific capability broadly accessible.

Blog post

Introducing Science Foundries, a new way for scientists and agents to work together ↗

Benchling's Head of AI, Nicholas Larus-Stone, launches Science Foundries: you pick a process such as protein production or lead optimization, then assign which steps agents run and where scientists must approve. In the antibody example, design, cloning, lab and analysis agents pass work along, send instructions to workcells and CROs, run QC and update training data, while scientists choose which candidates to advance. Parabilis Medicines is the named customer. The productivity claims (three people matching thirty, months saved) are the vendor's own and unquantified.3 The design choice that matters is the role assignment: agents run the steps and scientists hold the approval gates. That is the human actuation gate, sold as a product. What is missing is any independent check beyond QC and sign-off.

“We just launched Science Foundries at Benchtalk!” ↗

Nicholas Larus-Stone's launch post says Science Foundries let “anyone … orchestrate a team of agents to accomplish real scientific work”, from target ID and screening through assay development to medical writing. He credits “vibe coding, natively multiplayer agents (!!!), agent swarms, and of course good old fashioned harness engineering”, and says a Foundry is set up by chatting with Benchling AI. Benchmarking biopharma knowledge work ↗

Convoke benchmarks models on six biopharma work products outside R&D: target safety assessment, tumor landscape, shelved assets, catalyst calendar, clinical benchmark and commercial forecast. Domain experts wrote penalty-based rubrics (major, moderate, minor errors), and an LLM grader with research tools scored each criterion against a citation. They report that frontier OpenAI models lead and that models handle structured spreadsheet compilation well but struggle with narrative synthesis and judgment-heavy forecasts. Frontier models cost more than 3x as much per work product. The grader has not yet been validated against expert grading.5 Compiling a catalyst calendar is checkable. A commercial forecast needs design judgment, and that is where models slip: the taste gap again, in a pharma setting. The rubric grader is itself unvalidated, so treat the rankings as provisional.

Essay

The Labs Have the Best Models With Infinite Tokens, So Why Aren’t Their Products Better? ↗

Ben Davis defines taste as “the ability to make good decisions in places where there isn’t a programmatic verification function”. Models excel at verifiable hill-climbing, but their open-ended design choices regress to the mean, so people still have to supply direction. He calls taste “the only real stop” to recursive self-improvement.6 The real question: can a harness and a model together make good decisions where no programmatic verifier exists? “Make this function fast” is hill-climbing. Which direction to pursue, which design to ship and which claim to trust are taste.

A thread: forgetting to escape idea basins

Two papers ten days apart reach the same answer: long-running research agents get stuck refining their own past ideas, and the cure is to forget. Read Fork-and-Flush first, then the more aggressive version.

Preprint
The idea-basins paper

Fork-and-Flush: Escaping Idea Basins in Autoresearch Agents ↗

Cai, Papailiopoulos and colleagues find that independent runs of the same autoresearch agent on the same task end at very different scores, and that extra compute does not close the gap. Comparing solutions by what they do suggests each run stays inside a local “idea basin”. Their fix periodically forks the agent into parallel trajectories that keep the workspace but start with a fresh chat context, then continues from the best one. Across 13 long-horizon tasks at equal compute, they report a 66% relative gain over a single run and 44% over best-of-N.7 Think of a hiker who only ever steps uphill from where they stand: they reach the top of the nearest hill, not the highest one. A fresh context is a new starting point on the map.

The forgetting paper

Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting ↗

Tan, Dimakis, Zaharia and colleagues at Berkeley show that running an autoresearch agent longer, running agents in parallel, or letting them talk all plateau, because agents anchor on their growing history. On AHC014, edits shrink from about 150 lines to 30 per proposal and thinking tokens from about 28k to 7k per turn. Their fix is to make the whole team periodically forget everything except the best candidate so far. Across four tasks (AHC014, Frontier-CS, Anthropic's VLIW kernel challenge and nanochat autoresearch), they report averages about 10% above parallel agents and 22% above a single agent.8 Fork-and-Flush keeps the workspace and clears the chat. This paper clears both and keeps only the best answer. Shrinking edit sizes are a measurable symptom of an agent getting stuck.

Article

AgentRun: a harness for repetitive knowledge work ↗

Miguel Rios (Grep.ai) describes a harness in which a frontier agent first does a job from a written procedure, keeps notes and traces, then writes a typed workflow and “fires itself”. The workflow mixes agent steps, plain code and typed questions answered by a small calibrated model (Jev), under the rule “Jev decides what a thing is; code decides what follows.” A verify clause checks each claim against the evidence the agent cited. On an anti-money-laundering alert review, the author reports cost per alert falling from $2.89 to $0.25 and judged accuracy rising from 90% to 95%, scored on a held-out set they never looked at.14 A practical answer to the taste question. Closed decisions (“is this a match?”) become typed questions that a cheap calibrated model answers and code routes. Open judgment stays with the agent or a human. A held-out set guards against grading your own homework.

Jeff Dean in conversation with Dawn Song: from answering questions to making discoveries ↗

Dean's first public talk after leaving Google, at the AASF Frontier & Pioneer Symposium at Stanford, moderated by Dawn Song. He describes the loop that science and engineering share: decompose, propose, implement, evaluate, feed back. Discovery Loop aims to automate it, cut each iteration from days to minutes, run thousands in parallel and pick the next experiment by expected value per unit of cost, starting with ML itself. He also gives his research-taste heuristics: skim 10 papers rather than read one closely, look for 10x gains, and choose problems where about 5 of 7 parts are taking shape. The summary is built from auto-generated captions.29 Experiment A has a 10% chance of +5 and costs 1 GPU-day (EV/cost 0.5). B has a 50% chance of +2 and costs 4 GPU-days (0.25). Dean's rule runs A first. What pure EV/cost misses is what a cheap failure teaches you.
