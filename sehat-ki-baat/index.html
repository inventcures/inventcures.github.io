A Hindi health benchmark for LLMs

Sehat Ki Baat

सेहत की बात

Sehat Ki Baat tests whether a language model gives good health advice to people who ask in Hindi the way they actually type it: in Devanagari, in Roman letters on a phone, and in Hinglish. I'm building it now, and this page marks where it starts.

In development · first announced 24 November 2026

Why I'm building it

A man in Patna types to a chatbot: mere papa ko 3 din se bukhar hai aur ab saans lene mein dikkat ho rahi hai, kya karein? My father has had a fever for three days and now he's struggling to breathe, what should we do? The right answer is to get him to a hospital now. I want to know whether a model says that as readily in Hindi as it does in English.

In two studies this month I asked four open models the same health questions in English and in three forms of Hindi, and graded the answers against rubrics that physicians wrote. Three of the four gave clearly worse advice in every Hindi form. Romanized Hindi, the way most people type Hindi on a phone, scored lowest for every model on general questions.

The models weren't confused about what was asked. Their middle layers matched 93 to 100% of Hindi questions to the right English question. The grader wasn't marking Hindi down either: English translations of the Hindi answers scored no higher. The advice itself got worse. I wrote up what I can and can't say about why in Worse health advice outside English.

Most Hindi medical test sets I've found are multiple choice, in Devanagari, and machine translated from English exams. Multiple choice hides much of this gap, and almost nobody tests romanized Hindi or Hinglish. Sehat Ki Baat is meant to fill that hole.

What it tests

Every question comes in the forms people really use.

  • Open advice, not just letters. Most items ask for the kind of answer a worried family member needs, graded against a rubric: does it spot the red flag, say when to go to hospital, avoid unsafe advice. A multiple-choice track sits alongside, so the two can be compared.
  • India's disease burden first. Topics are weighted by what makes people in India and other low- and middle-income countries sick: heart disease and stroke, diabetes and high blood pressure, tuberculosis, maternal and newborn health, fever and infections such as dengue and malaria, anaemia and nutrition, breathing problems, mental health, snakebite and injuries.
  • A separate cancer track. Cancer gets its own set, weighted toward the cancers India sees most, such as mouth, breast, cervix and lung, along with tobacco, screening, late diagnosis, treatment side effects and palliative care.
  • Checked by people. Native speakers check every Hindi form, and clinicians check the questions and rubrics, so a score reflects the model and not a clumsy translation.
  • Open and closed models. The open models from my studies already have results on a first version. The benchmark will be run on closed models too.

Status

The design is in progress, drawing on the two studies above and a survey of existing Hindi and Indic medical benchmarks. Questions, code and results will come out with the paper. Until then they're available on request.
